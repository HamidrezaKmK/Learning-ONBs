Learning Orthonormal Bases for Function Spaces

Hamidreza Kamkari, Sina Nabizadeh, Justin Solomon

MIT CSAIL · NeurIPS 2026

Paper Code BibTeX

Summary

TL;DR Orthonormal bases such as Fourier series or wavelets are fixed: they do not adapt to the data or the problem. We parameterize an infinite, continuous and exactly orthonormal basis with a neural network, as the endpoint of a learned path of rotations that starts at a reference basis such as Fourier, and optimize it by gradient descent for the task at hand.

Every point of the sphere $\mathrm{SO}(\infty)$ is a whole orthonormal basis. Starting from the same 2-D Fourier basis, three parameter settings $\theta_1,\theta_3,\theta_2$ of one architecture trace three paths: to the principal functions of 1-D signals, of MNIST digits, and the eigenfaces of CelebA. The animation is computed from our trained checkpoints: every card is a real basis function, shown step by step along the learned path from the Fourier basis to the learned one.

Why learn a basis?

Fixed bases do not adapt

Fourier or wavelets are the same for every problem. On real data the energy spreads over many coefficients, and at discontinuities Fourier rings (the Gibbs phenomenon), so truncation is wasteful.

Grid methods inherit the grid

Classical PCA and eigensolvers discretize the problem first. The result is a finite set of vectors on that grid, limited by its resolution (the Nyquist limit), and has to be interpolated to be used anywhere else.

Neural eigensolvers stay finite

Existing neural approaches learn continuous functions but usually only a handful of them, and need explicit orthogonalization or penalties to keep them orthonormal.

Our approach. Every orthonormal basis is a rotation $Q$ of a reference one, and every rotation is the end of a path $\tfrac{d}{dt}Q=K_\theta(t)\,Q$ driven by a skew-adjoint generator. We model $K_\theta$ with a neural network of finite rank and integrate with Cayley steps. The result is

  • exactly orthonormal for every value of the weights, with no constraint to enforce;
  • infinite and continuous: every basis function can be evaluated at any point, at any resolution;
  • expressive: we prove that even rank-2 generators reach a dense set of rotations (those $e^{K}$ with Hilbert–Schmidt $K$).

Experiments

The same architecture, starting from the Fourier basis, adapts to three kinds of task: PCA, operator eigenproblems and dynamics. The figures are interactive: hover the charts for values, and use the controls to step through settings.

PCA Functional PCA on 1-D signals with a discontinuity

Signals are random sines with a jump at $x=0.5$, which is hard for Fourier. We learn the principal functions of this distribution (its Karhunen–Loève basis). The learned basis beats finite, grid-based PCA from about 10 basis functions on, and has no Nyquist floor. With about 100 functions its reconstruction error is an order of magnitude lower than Fourier's.

PCA Eigen-digits and eigenfaces, without a grid

For MNIST stored as implicit neural representations (each digit is a SIREN network), the data itself has no grid; for CelebA we use quadrature on a 64×64 grid. The Fourier basis turns into principal functions of each dataset, and the first few learned functions capture far more of the signal energy than the same number of Fourier functions.

Eigenproblems Diagonalizing a neural tangent kernel

Beyond PCA, the same objective diagonalizes other linear operators. Here we track the eigenfunctions of the NTK of a two-moons classifier during training. A grid eigensolver gets the global structure but pixelates; the learned basis recovers the same leading eigenfunctions and also resolves the fine detail at the decision boundary.

Dynamics An energy-preserving Koopman operator for a fluid

For volume-preserving flows the Koopman operator $f\mapsto f\circ\Psi$ is orthogonal, so it matches our parameterization. We fit $Q_\theta$ to one time step of a Taylor–Green vortex and roll it out. The rollout is noisier than the Runge–Kutta transports, but it conserves $L^2$ energy exactly, while RK1, RK2 and RK4 drift (RK2 gains 28% and RK4 loses 6% by $t=4$).
